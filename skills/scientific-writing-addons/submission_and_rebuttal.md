# Submission, Editorial Assessment, Rebuttal, and Appeals

Practical guidance for navigating the editorial and peer-review process at
high-impact journals (Nature family and similar). Use this reference when
preparing a manuscript for submission, choosing an article format, writing the
title/abstract for editorial appeal, responding to referee reports, or deciding
whether to appeal a rejection.

---

## 1. Preparation: Before You Submit

### Choose the right article type

Select the format that best fits the length, novelty, and mechanistic depth of
the study. Matching the format to the work is itself an editorial signal.

| Format | Length | Main figures | Required profile |
|--------|--------|--------------|------------------|
| **Article** | ~6000 words | 8 | High novelty **and** high mechanistic development |
| **Letter** | ~3000 words | 4 | High novelty **and** moderate mechanistic development |
| **Brief Communication** | ~1500 words | 2 | Highly interesting findings with less extensive mechanistic development |
| **Resource** | ~6000 words | 8 | Judged on resource value, reusability, and mechanistic development |

When advising an author, recommend the smallest format that fully carries the
story: a tightly-scoped finding reads as stronger as a Letter or Brief
Communication than as a padded Article.

### Craft a strong title and abstract

- **Title**: accurate, concise, clear, and engaging. It should draw the reader
  in without overstating. Avoid vague constructions; name the advance.
- **Abstract**: must accomplish three things — (i) explain the study's
  **rationale**, (ii) state **all** the main findings, and (iii) outline the
  **broader implications**. The classic Nature formula — *"Here, we show..."* —
  is a useful template for the central-result sentence.
- **Why it matters**: a strong title and abstract are decisive not only for the
  editor's triage decision but for **convincing busy reviewers to accept the
  invitation to review** the paper at all. Treat them as recruitment tools.

## 2. Initial Editorial Assessment

Before sending a paper out for peer review, editors weigh a set of core criteria.
Anticipate these in the cover letter and the framing of the introduction and
discussion.

**Core criteria:**
- Conceptual novelty (genuinely new ideas, not incremental restatement)
- Degree of advance over the existing literature
- Significance of the findings
- Breadth of interest across the journal's audience
- Relevance to the journal's specific readership
- Strong data that actually support the stated conclusions

**Study-specific criteria** (field-dependent — emphasize whichever apply):
- Translational or clinical relevance
- Deep mechanistic insight
- Resource value (for Resource-type submissions)
- Technological or methodological advance
- Policy implications

A useful self-check before submission: for each conclusion in the abstract, can
you point to the specific figure/panel and statistic that supports it? Editors
reject most often on weak support and over-interpretation (see
`writing_principles.md`).

## 3. Addressing Referee Reports (The Rebuttal)

When the manuscript returns with reviews, construct an effective, point-by-point
response. A well-built rebuttal is frequently the difference between acceptance
and rejection at revision.

**Formatting:**
- Copy the reviewer reports **in full** — do not paraphrase or selectively quote.
- Use color or italics to clearly distinguish the reviewers' comments from your
  replies (e.g., reviewer text in one color/italic, author response in plain).
- Address every point with its own numbered reply.

**Clarity:**
- Embed relevant figures **directly within the rebuttal document** so reviewers
  can follow your argument without hunting through the manuscript.
- Include the editor's guidance in your response, and show how each requested
  change was handled.

**Tone and diplomacy:**
- Treat every critique as an opportunity to improve the paper, not an attack.
- Stay diplomatic, tactful, and focused on the science.
- Where you choose **not** to make a requested change, say so explicitly and
  explain the reasoning clearly and respectfully — do not silently omit it.

**Scope issues:**
- If you are unable or unwilling to expand the study's scope as a reviewer
  requests, it may be better to **transfer the manuscript to another journal**
  rather than force a mismatch. The editor can often help facilitate a transfer.

## 4. Handling Rejections and Appeals

Appeals are only worth making — and only considered — when the **core concerns
that caused the rejection can be objectively resolved**.

**Valid reasons to appeal:**
- You have generated **new, additional data** that addresses an issue the
  editor/reviewers identified as essential.
- There were **objective, factual errors** in the reviewer reports or the
  editor's comments.
- You have **specific, concrete evidence of reviewer bias** (not a hunch).

**When NOT to appeal:**
- Do **not** appeal over subjective disagreements — e.g., arguing the editor
  underrated the paper's "novelty" or "significance." These are judgment calls
  and will not be reversed by re-assertion.
- Do **not** disguise an appeal as a revision. A rejection is not an implicit
  invitation to revise and resubmit; submitting a revised manuscript in place of
  a proper appeal wastes everyone's time.

Keep appeals brief, factual, and tied to the specific, resolvable concern.

---

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Article type matches the length, novelty, and mechanistic depth of the work
- [ ] Title is accurate, concise, and engaging
- [ ] Abstract states rationale + all main findings + broader implications ("Here, we show...")
- [ ] Every abstract claim is traceable to a figure/panel + statistic
- [ ] Cover letter speaks to the core editorial criteria (novelty, advance, significance, breadth, fit, data strength)
- [ ] Rebuttal copies reviewer text in full, color-coded, point-by-point
- [ ] Rebuttal embeds figures inline and addresses editorial guidance
- [ ] Declined points are explained, not omitted; tone stays diplomatic
- [ ] Any appeal targets an objectively resolvable concern (new data / factual error / concrete bias evidence), not a subjective disagreement
